\begin{abstract}
High-precision photometric observations have revealed a new ubiquitous phenomena in early type stars: stochastic low-frequency photometric variability. 
Possible explanations for the origin of this photometric variability include internal gravity waves launched by the convective core, or the presence of subsurface convection.  Here we show theoretical predictions for the trend of characteristic frequency and amplitude of the perturbation induced by subsurface convection in massive stars, and compare these predictions to observations. We found that both the characteristic frequency $\nu_{\rm char}$ and amplitude $\alpha_0$ of the observed photometric variability correlate very well with properties in the iron subsurface convection zone. While the amplitude of photometric variability also correlates with theoretical predictions of the flux of internal gravity waves generated by core convection, the observed trend of characteristic frequency is at odds with theoretical expectations from convective turnover timescales in convective cores. 
%This tentatively breaks the degeneracy between the two plausible models in favor of subsurface convection. 
We suggest a unified picture in which low-frequency photometric variability, surface turbulence, magnetic spots and wind variability in massive stars could all have the same origin: the presence of subsurface convection zones.

\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}\label{sec:introduction}
Massive stars largely drive the dynamical and chemical evolution of gas in galaxies. They accomplish this via their stellar winds, eruptions, and explosive deaths. The legacy of massive stars is continued by the compact remnants they leave behind, neutron stars and black holes. These remnants can merge and generate the gravitational waves observed by LIGO/Virgo. The evolutionary trajectory starting with a massive star burning hydrogen in its core and ending with a compact remnant is understood only qualitatively. We still do not know how to map initial properties of the star, like mass, rotation rate, and metallicity, to e.g. the final mass and spin of the compact remnant it leaves behind. The picture is actually even more complicated, as the majority of massive stars is found in multiple systems \citep{Sana:2012},  with a large fraction expected to interact with a companion during its lifetime \citep{deMink:2014}. 

The path towards a detailed understanding of massive stars begins with a quantitative study of their internal structure on the main sequence. In recent years asteroseismology has opened a new window to the interior of these challenging astrophysical environments, with high precision photometry from space delivering many new exciting results \citep[e.g., MOST, CoRoT, BRITE, Kepler/K2 and TESS, see ][]{BowmanReview:2020}.
The latest discovery is the detection of a new ubiquitous phenomena in massive stars, stochastic low-frequency photometric variability \citep{2019NatAs...3..760B,Bowman:2019,Bowman:2020}. 
This phenomena joins a number of other surface and wind phenomena that are routinely observed in early-type stars, including surface turbulence \citep{Simon-Diaz:2014}, line profile variability \citep{Fullerton:1996}, and discrete absorption components in UV spectra \citep{Howarth:1989,Cranmer:1996,Fullerton:1997,Kaper:1997}. Other phenomena that are harder to observe but could still be common in these intrinsically bright stars are surface magnetism and bright spots \citep[e.g.][]{Ramiaramanantsoa:2014}.

The origin of this stochastic photometric variability is currently debated. It could be caused by internal gravity waves (IGWs) launched by turbulent core convection \citep{Edelmann:2019,Ratnasingam:2020}, or by sub-surface convection zones \citep{Cantiello:2009,Blomme:2011,Lecoanet:2020}. Regardless of its origin, this photometric signal is likely carrying important information about the stellar structure, complementing asteroseismic studies that use well-identified oscillation modes \citep[e.g.][]{Aerts:2019,Aerts_araa:2019,BowmanReview:2020}.

A recent development was the use of high resolution ground-based spectroscopy for the targets observed by K2 and TESS \citep{Bowman:2020}. This allowed precise determination of stellar parameters, including mass, luminosity and a measure of surface velocity fluctuations (macroturbulence). Here we used this data to compare the observed properties of the stochastic photometric variability with some predictions coming from 1D stellar models. 
We discuss the properties of subsurface convection, in particular the expected trends in amplitude and frequency of the variability they could induce at the stellar surface, as a function of the stellar temperature and luminosity. We show that the predicted trends coming from a suburface convection zone driven by the iron opacity peak at $\approx150kK $ match very well the observations.

\section{Methods}
We calculated stellar evolution models using the Modules for Experiments in Stellar Astrophysics
\citep[MESA][]{Paxton2011, Paxton2013, Paxton2015, Paxton2018, Paxton2019} software instrument.
Details on the microphysics inputs to this software instrument are given in Appendix~\ref{sec:software}. 
The inlists used to run our models can be found in this \href{https://github.com/matteocantiello/rednoise}{GitHub repository}\footnote{https://github.com/matteocantiello/rednoise}.
We focused on the structure and convective properties of our models, in order to calculate typical frequencies and convective fluxes. 
Convection is calculated in the framework of the Mixing Length Theory \citep[][MLT hereafter]{1958ZA.....46..108B}, and we adopted $\alphamlt = 1.6$. While in efficient convection zones (e.g. stellar cores) the choice of this parameter does not change results much, this is not true in superadiabatic, inefficient convective regions close to the stellar surface \citep{2015ApJ...813...74J,Cantiello:2019}. We discuss in Section~\ref{sec:discussion} how uncertainties in the choice of the $\alphamlt$  parameter affect our results. 

Since we are interested in the excitation of surface phenomena, we focus on the upper part of convective zones and following \citet{Cantiello:2009} we define the average of a quantity  $\overline{q}$ as
\begin{equation}
\overline{q} \equiv \frac{1}{\alphamlt \hp} \int_{\Rtop-\alphamlt\hp}^{\Rtop} q(r)\; dr
\label{aver}
\end{equation}
where  $\hp$ is the pressure scale height calculated at the upper boundary ($\Rtop$) of the 
convective zone of interest.  We tested using different average procedures, and found that our results do not depend much on the specific choice.  

Using this definition, we extracted the average Mach number $\macha$, convective velocity $\vca$ and density $\rhoa$ in the convective core and in the subsurface convection zones of our models.
The most important subsurface convection zone for the massive stars discussed in this work is the iron convection zone (FeCZ) \citep{Cantiello:2009}, although in the low luminosity regime He-driven convection zones could play a role as well \citep{Cantiello:2019}. 
%For each stellar model we evaluated the average convective velocity and convective turnover timescale in subsurface convection zones. 
We define the characteristic frequency as 
\begin{equation}\label{eq:nuchar}
\nuchar \equiv \frac{1}{2\pi\tau},
\end{equation}
where $\tau$ is a characteristic timescale. When calculating the characteristic frequency in our models, we set $\tau$ to be the  average convective turnover time 
\begin{equation}\label{eq:turnover}
\turnover = \alphamlt \hpa / \vca 
\end{equation}
calculated either in the FeCZ or in the convective core.  
We proceeded to calculate convective turnover timescales and other relevant properties like convective fluxes $F_{\rm c} = \rhoa \vca^3$. 
We did this as a function of both mass and evolutionary history for stars ranging from $5-120 M_\odot$.
Here we present results for an initial metallicity of Z=0.02, but in Appendix~\ref{appen:grids} we report results for model grids with Z = 0.014, 0.006 and 0.002 as well.

We compare these quantities with the observed characteristic frequency $\nucharobs$ and amplitude $\alphazero$ of stochastic photometric variability. These quantities are derived by fitting the stochastically-excited low-frequency power excess in a power
density spectrum using a Lorentzian function \citep[e.g.][]{Bowman:2019}:

\begin{equation}\label{eq:rednoise}
\alpha(\nu) = \frac{\alphazero}{1+\big(\frac{\nu}{\nucharobs}\big)^{\gamma}} \, + P_{\rm W}.
\end{equation}
This shows that $\alphazero$ represents the amplitude at zero
frequency. $\nucharobs$ is defined as in Eq.~\ref{eq:nuchar} with $\tau$ the typical timescale of the stochastic photometric variability.  
Finally, $\gamma$ is the gradient of the linear part of the profile and $P_{\rm W}$ is a white noise term.

\section{Results}
We want to test a possible correlation between the properties of the FeCZ and observed photospheric phenomena, in particular photometric variability and turbulent velocities at the stellar surface (macroturbulence). If photometric variability was caused by the FeCZ, a natural choice of proxy for the typical timescale of this variability is the convective turnover timescale.  We found typical values of the convective turnover timescale to be about $\sim 0.1\dots2$ d in the FeCZ, with a tendency for shorter values in models with high effective temperature and surface luminosity \citep{Cantiello:2009}. To quantitatively compare to the characteristic frequencies of the observed stochastic photometric variability in massive stars, we proceed mapping our theoretical predictions for the quantity $\nuchar$ (eq.~\ref{eq:nuchar}) on a spectroscopic H-R diagram \citep{2014A&A...564A..52L}. This reveals a very good agreement, with our models reproducing both the typical values of the observed $\nuchar$ and the trend with  $\logl$ and $\logteff$ (Fig.~\ref{fig:nuchar_fecz}). 
%This is especially true given the uncertainties in the convective velocities predictions from MLT (Sec.~\ref{sec:mlt}) the agreement is very good, 
While the characteristic frequencies found by \citet{Bowman:2020} seem to be larger by a factor of $\approx$ 3, our predictions for the turnover timescale are affected by  uncertainty in the convective velocities arising from the MLT treatment ($\vc \propto \alphamlt^{3}$, so $\nuchar \propto \alphamlt^{2}$). Since $\alphamlt$ is uncertain by a factor of 2 or so, our estimates of the characteristic frequency in the FeCZ are uncertain by a factor of  $\approx$ 4.



We proceed with mapping quantities that measure the amplitude of perturbations at the location of the FeCZ. We then check if some of these properties correlate with the amplitude of observed photospheric phenomena, including turbulent velocity fluctuations and photometric variability.  While here we are agnostic about the particular mechanism, we assume that some physical process connects the subsurface layers to the photosphere. We  discuss  the most likely candidates for this process in Sec.~\ref{sec:discussion}.
We show the average convective velocity in the FeCZ in Fig.~\ref{fig:vconv}. Velocities of the order $10 \dots 100\ \kms$ are found across the upper H-R diagram, with a trend of increasing $\vc$ for higher luminosities. This trend is similar to what is observed for the macroturbulent velocity field at the surface of massive stars. This  is in agreement with the results of \citet{Grassitelli:2015}, who found a tight correlation between the turbulent pressure (P$_{\rm turb} \propto \vc^2$) in the FeCZ  and spectroscopically-derived macroturbulent velocities in massive stars. Interestingly, the range of the velocity field in the FeCZ is also similar to the observed range of macroturbulent velocities. Again the caveat is the large uncertainty (a factor $\approx 10$) of the convective velocities calculated using the MLT in inefficient convective zones \citep{Cantiello:2019}. We plot the average Mach number $\macha$ of convection in the FeCZ in Fig.~\ref{fig:mach}, showing that contrary to core convection the FeCZ is just mildly subsonic, with values of the Mach number ranging from 0.01 to 0.3. The relative convective flux in these regions is shown in Fig.~\ref{fig:nuchar_fecz}, where we also report the  the observed amplitude of photometric variability. Values of F$_{\rm c}$/F$_{*}$ tend to increase with increasing $\logl$ and decreasing $\logteff$, a trend that is also found for the values of 
observed photometric variability.

Next, we focus on the characteristic timescale of waves excited by core convection. 
%The characteristic angular frequency of the waves is the angular convective
%turnover frequency $\omega_{c} = \vc/\lambda$ near the radiative-convective interface, where $\lambda$ is the mixing length and $\vc$ is the convective velocity.
We expect the maximum of the IGW flux to be launched at frequencies close to $\nuchar = (2\pi\tau_{\rm c})^{-1}$, with $\tau_{\rm c}$ defined in Eq.~\ref{eq:turnover}.  Fig.~\ref{fig:nuchar_core} shows that the predicted values of $\nuchar$ are in the range $0.02\dots0.008\,{\rm  d}^{-1}$ ($0.2\dots0.08\, \muhz$), in agreement with the results of \citet{Shiode:2013}. These values are about 2 orders of magnitude smaller than the typical characteristic frequencies of stochastic photometric variability observed by \citep{Bowman:2019,2019NatAs...3..760B}.  Wave propagation through the stellar envelope affects the spectrum, changing the frequency of maximum power of waves reaching the surface \citep{Lecoanet:2020}. Radiative damping efficiently suppresses low-frequency IGWs (because these waves have high radial wavenumbers), so that the the peak of the spectrum observed at the surface is expected to move to higher frequencies. The amplitude of this effect depends on the envelope properties, which are a function of $\logteff$ and $\logl$. Nevertheless, let's assume that the trends of predicted $\nuchar$ as function of $\logteff$ and $\logl$ is mostly determined by the core convective properties and not by radiative damping. 
Under this assumption, Fig.~\ref{fig:nuchar_core} shows that the expectation is that $\nuchar$ should increase with both $\logteff$ and $\logl$, so that the characteristic frequencies of peak IGWs should increase as stars evolve on the main sequence. This is exactly the opposite of what is observed:  the characteristic frequencies of stochastic photometric variability is largest for stars early on on the main sequence, and seem to decreases as stars evolve.  
Therefore either radiative damping is able to revert this trend, or the observed variability might not be caused by IGWs launched by the core. 

 
 
\subsection{Macroturbulence}

The hypothesis that subsurface convection is responsible for surface turbulence is corroborated by the match between trends in the FeCZ properties (i.e. convective velocities and turbulent pressure) and the observed micro and macroturbulence \citep{Cantiello:2009,Grassitelli:2015}.
In particular, there appears to be a critical magnetic field above which macroturbulence disappears, and this critical field strength corresponds closely to the critical field needed to stabilize the FeCZ~\citep{2019MNRAS.487.3904M,10.1093/mnras/stt921,Jermyn:2020}.

We can analogously define a critical magnetic field strength $B_{\rm crit}$ which suffices to reflect IGWs before they reach the photosphere.
Using the dispersion relation for IGWs in a magentic medium, the radial component of this field is~\citep{2015Sci...350..423F}
\begin{align}
	B_{r, \rm crit} = \frac{\omega}{2 k_r}\sqrt{4\pi \rho},
\end{align}
where $\omega=\nu/2\pi$ is the angular frequency of the waves, $\rho$ is the density, and $k_r$ is the radial wave-number.
For IGWs the radial wave-number is related to the azimuthal quantum number $\ell$ by
\begin{align}\label{eq:kr}
	k_r \approx \frac{\sqrt{\ell(\ell+1)}}{r}\left(\frac{N}{\omega}\right),
\end{align}
where $N$ is the \brvs\ frequency.
So
\begin{align}
	\label{eq:Brcrit}
	B_{r, \rm crit} &\approx \frac{\omega^2 r}{N}\sqrt{\frac{4\pi \rho}{2\ell(\ell+1)}} \\
	&\approx \frac{\omega^2 r}{\ell N}\sqrt{4\pi \rho}.
\end{align}
Note that because this decreases with increasing $\ell$, all waves of a given frequency are reflected if that with $\ell=1$ is reflected.

We computed $B_{r, \rm crit}$ for several values of $\ell$ and $\nu$ for a main-sequence model of a $30 M_\odot$ star as a function of radius, shown in Fig.~\ref{fig:macro}.
For frequencies comparable to those of core convection the critical magnetic field strength is of order $10^{-2}\,\mathrm{G}$ to $10^{-1}\,\mathrm{G}$.
Strong macroturbulence is observed in similar-mass O-type stars with magnetic fields up to $2.5\,\mathrm{kG}$ (e.g. HD~191612)~\citep{10.1093/mnras/stt921}, so macroturbulence in those stars is unlikely to be due to IGW coming from their cores if those IGW have similar frequencies to that of core convection.

For frequencies comparable to the observed $\nu_{\rm char}$, on the order of $3\,\mathrm{d}^{-1}$, the critical magnetic field is much larger, on the order of $300\,\mathrm{G}$ to $1\,\mathrm{kG}$.
This makes an explanation of macroturbulence based on IGW marginally inconsistent with observations showing strong macroturbulence up to field strengths of $2.5\,\mathrm{kG}$.
However, because equation~\eqref{eq:Brcrit} is a strong function of $\omega$, and hence of $\nu$, it is possible that these strongly magnetized stars just have larger $\nu_{\rm char}$.
For instance if HD~191612 has $\nu_{\rm char} \approx 5\,\mathrm{d}^{-1}$ then it would have $B_{r,\rm crit} \approx 3\,\mathrm{kG}$ for the $\ell=1$ mode.

A further prediction of this calculation is that, if IGW are the cause of macroturbulence in these stars, we should expect the strength of macroturbulence to decline with increasing magnetic field strength as more and more modes are reflected before they reach the surface.
\citet{10.1093/mnras/stt921} find no such trend, and the only strong correlation they see of macroturbulence with magnetic field strength is that it vanishes when the magnetic field exceeds the FeCZ shutoff strength.
This again points against an explanation of macroturbulence based on IGWs.

\section{Discussion}\label{sec:discussion}\label{sec:discussion}

%\subsection{Above the Iron Convection Zone}\label{sec:connecting_mechanisms}
The presence of a subsurface convection zone can result in variability of photospheric properties via a number of processes, including wave excitation \citep{Cantiello:2009,Grassitelli:2015} and magnetic buoyancy \citep{Cantiello:2011}. Moreover, multi dimensional simulations including radiation have been performed in a restricted range of the parameter space, and show that the full turbulent manifestation of these convective regions extends up to the stellar surface \citep{2015ApJ...813...74J,Jiang:2017,2018Natur.561..498J}. Therefore it could be that the observed stochastic photometric variability simply represents the direct manifestation of turbulent, radiation-dominated convection at the stellar surface.  
The simplified MLT treatment in our one dimensional calculations is unable to capture the complex phenomenology of these layers, but the fact that it can reproduce both the  timescales and the trends in amplitude of the observed photometric variabilty is compelling. It calls for radiation hydrodynamics simulations covering the parameter space of the TESS observations in order to unravel the precise mechanism connecting subsurface convection zones to the observed surface variability. 


The results of \citep{Bowman:2019} show that low-frequency stochastic photometric variability is also observed in low-metallicity, LMC stars.  
The presence of the FeCZ depends on the luminosity and metallicity of the star, and in 1D stellar evolution calculations the FeCZ occurs above a luminosity of L$\approx10^{3.2} L_{\odot}$ for Z=0.02.
This corresponds to a zero age main sequence star of about 7$\mso$ (see e.g. Fig.~\ref{fig:macroturbulence}). The LMC has a metallicity about half solar, and in models at Z=0.008 the FeCZ only appears at L$\approx10^{3.9} L_{\odot}$, corresponding to a zero age main sequence star of about 11$\mso$ \citep{Cantiello:2009}. Note that these limits could move downward  in the presence of atomic diffusion and radiative acceleration \citep{Richer:2000}, or due to an upward revision of the uncertain values of Fe opacity \citep[e.g.][]{Bailey:2015}. It will be interesting to see if the TESS observations can probe the transition region between stars with and without a FeCZ, and determine a possible change in surface properties. 

It is important to note that some low-luminosity, main sequence A stars in \citep{Bowman:2019} show low-frequency, stochastic photometric variability. For these stars models do not predict the presence of the FeCZ. However, these stars still show (sub)surface convection zones triggered by ionization of H and He \citep{Cantiello:2019}.  The characteristic frequencies of convection in these regions are also in the range $\sim$tens of $\mu$Hz, although the  amplitude of the velocity fluctuations they can induce is much smaller than for the FeCZ, so it is not clear if they could be linked to observed photometric variability and macroturbulence. 

This said it's clear that the regime of early-A and late-B type stars is the most favorable for the detection of core-generated IGWs. The relative perturbation induced by subsurface convection is weakest in this regime \citep{Cantiello:2019,Jermyn:2020}, and depending on the amplitude of core generated IGWs it could well be subdominant, allowing for a detection. 

\subsection{Red noise in evolved stars}
Stochastic photometric variability with similar properties to the massive main sequence stars discussed by \citep{Bowman:2019,Bowman:2020} was recently observed in evolved massive stars.
\citet{Naze:2021} detected red noise in both luminous blue variables (LBV) and Wolf-Rayet (WR) stars, and \citet{Dorn:2020} found the same photometric signature in yellow supergiants (YSG).  
LBV, WR and YSG are known to correspond to later stages of evolution. In particular, WR stars and YSG  are likely burning helium in their cores. 
The internal structure of OB, LBV, WR and YSG stars changes dramatically. This is expected to substantially affect the generation and propagation of internal gravity waves. In particular, the amount of radiative damping is expected to change due to the sheer differences in envelope temperature and densities.  We recall here that the radiative damping rate for a traveling g-mode is given by
\begin{align}\label{eqn:gammarad}
	&\rm \gamma_{rad} (\omega, \ell, r) = K_{\rm rad} (r) \, k_{r}^{2},\\ \label{eqn:Krad}
	&K_{\rm rad} (r) = \frac{16 \, \sigma \, T(r)^{3}}{3 \, \rho(r)^{2} \kappa(r) \, c_{p}(r)}, \\ \label{eqn:kr}
%	&k_{r} \approx \frac{\Lambda}{r} \frac{N}{\omega}.
\end{align}
%where $\Lambda^{2} \equiv \ell \, (\ell + 1)$ and $k_{r}$ is the radial wavenumber and the approximation in eqn. \ref{eqn:kr} relies on the WKB dispersion relation for g-modes ($\omega \ll  N, S_{\ell}$). 
where $k_{r}$ is the radial wavenumber (eqn.~\ref{eq:kr}) and $\kappa$ is the opacity.
Compact WR stars have surface temperatures that can exceed $\approx 10^5$K, while OB stars and LBVs have effective temperatures $\approx 10^{4.7\dots4}$K, with LBVs found at the cooler end of this range.  The surface of extended, low-density, YSGs is cooler than $10^4$K.  With different types of core convection (H-burning vs He-burning) and radiative damping properties, it would be surprising if core-generated IGW in e.g. OB and WR stars showed up at the surface with similar properties. On the other hand, the FeCZ is present below/at the surface in both OB and WR stars, and with similar velocities and convective turnover times. 

The discussion is a bit more complicated for LBVs and YSGs, since at lower temperatures other convective regions can become prominent \citep[Driven by H and He recombination, see e.g. ][]{2018Natur.561..498J}. We plan to systematically study the properties of (sub)surface convection in these evolved, cool stars in future work.  \citet{Dorn:2020} ruled out a (near) surface origin for the observed red noise in YSG, on the ground that the observed timescale do not follow the predicted scaling for granulation \citep{Kallinger:2014}. The Kallinger-scaling is applicable to granulation driven by surface convection, but in the case of subsurface convection the timescale of the perturbation can vary in complicated ways with the surface properties  \citep[see e.g. Fig.B.1 in][]{Grassitelli:2015b}. Therefore the argument of \citet{Dorn:2020} is not directly applicable, and  subsurface convective regions can not be ruled out  \citep{Lecoanet:2020}.

\subsection{Towards a unified model for surface phenomena in massive stars}
The presence of subsurface convection can simultaneously account for a large variety of puzzling phenomena that appear ubiquitous at the surface of early-type stars.
First we have microturbulence and macroturbulence, and both can be accounted for by velocity fields excited by the underlying FeCZ subsurface convection zone \citep{Cantiello:2009,Grassitelli:2015}. The only stars with no macroturbulence appear to have magnetic fields strong enough to shut off convection in the FeCZ, while stars with slightly lower surface magnetic fields show normal values of macroturbulence.  
Then we have the observation of  bright spots in early-type stars \citep[e.g.][]{Ramiaramanantsoa:2014}, which can be explained with the presence of magnetic spots rising from subsurface convective layers \citep{Cantiello:2011,Cantiello:2019}. These photospheric magnetic spots can then be responsible for another ubiquitous phenomena in early-type stars, the presence of discrete absorption components (DACs) in their UV spectra \citep[e.g.][]{Howarth:1989,Cranmer:1996,Fullerton:1997,Kaper:1997}. 
Overall subsurface convection is expected to drive velocity and density perturbations at the stellar surface \citep[e.g.][]{Cantiello:2009}. These perturbations could explain the ubiquitous line profile variability \citep{Fullerton:1996} and provide a seed for the rapid development of wind instabilities that lead to wind clumping \citep[e.g.][]{Owocki:1988,Sundqvist:2013}.
Finally, these surface velocity and density perturbations  would necessarily induce photometric variability, and we have shown here that the observed low-frequency stochastic noise detected in the majority of massive stars correlates very well with the presence and properties of the FeCZ. 

An economical hypothesis emerges: the presence of subsurface convection, and in particular of the FeCZ, could be the common underlying physical cause for the appearance of turbulence, magnetic spots, low-frequency photometric variability, as well as wind and spectroscopic variability in early-type stars.

\section{Conclusions}

We used one-dimensional stellar evolution calculations to predict trends for the timescale and amplitude of surface photometric variability induced by subsurface and core convection in the HR-Diagram.
The observed trends in amplitude are similar to the theoretical expectations for both the flux of IGWs coming from a convective core, and the relative convective flux in the FeCZ. The typical turnover time of convection in this subsurface convection zone matches very well both the observed timescales of variability and its trend in the spectroscopic HR-Diagram.
However, this is not the case for the trend of convective turnover time in the stellar core, which is opposite to the observations. 
The data point towards the presence of the FeCZ as the underlying physical process for the observed stochastic photometric variability. 

Similar to previous works, we  show that the observed trends in stellar macroturbulence are also well reproduced assuming the FeCZ as its driver. This connection is also supported by the observations of strongly magnetized early-type stars,
showing no macroturbulence only for magnetic fields above the critical value required to shut-off turbulent convection in the FeCZ. We find that IGWs coming from the stellar core would be reflected or damped for values of the magnetic field well below this critical value. The fact that stars with strong but subcritical magnetic fields show typical values of macroturbulence points against a convective core origin of this surface perturbation.

Overall the observations support a picture in which subsurface convection, and in particular the FeCZ, is responsible for a number of surface phenomena observed in early-type stars, including bright spots, surface turbulence, wind and spectroscopic variabilty, as well as stochastic photometric variability. Radiation (magneto)hydrodynamics simulations of the outer envelope regions of early-type stars are required to understand the details of how  subsurface convection zones cause the observed surface perturbations.

\section{Radial Displacement from IGWs}
\label{appen:xir}
Let's consider traveling internal gravity waves in the WKB approximation. 
The wave flux  is  $F_{\rm IGW} = \frac{1}{2} \rho \, |u|^2 u_{g,r}$
 with the group velocity $u_{g,r} = \partial \omega/\partial \kappa $.
Since  $u_{g,r} = (\omega/N)^2 \sqrt{N^2 - \omega^2} / k_h$,  $|u|^2 = (N/\omega)^2 u_r^2$, and 
$u_r = \omega \xi_r$ we obtain: 
\begin{equation}
F_{\rm IGW} = \frac{1}{2} \,\rho\, \xi_r^2 \omega^2 \frac{\sqrt{N^2-\omega^2}}{k_h}.
\end{equation}
Using  $k_h = \sqrt{\ell(\ell+1)}/r$ we can then write the relative radial displacement as:
\begin{equation}
\frac{\xi_r}{r} = \left[ \frac{F_{\rm IGW} \sqrt{\ell(\ell+1)}}{1/2 \rho \,r^3 \omega^2 \sqrt{N^2-\omega^2}} \right]^{1/2} \propto \sqrt{\frac{F_{\rm IGW}}{F_0}}
\end{equation}
